The short version

  1. Your browser encrypts each file with AES-256-GCM before upload. We store ciphertext and never receive the key.
  2. The key lives in the link after the #, in a password only you know, in your locker, or sealed inside a Tari payment to one address. Never with us.
  3. You write the exit: once, a number of opens, a time, or until you delete it. Our server enforces it and deletes the ciphertext on cue.
  4. Every exit goes on the public record on the Ootle.
  5. Your locker is a key your browser makes. No email, no phone, no password with us.

What happens when you drop a file

  1. If it's a photo (JPEG, PNG or WebP), your browser first removes location and camera data from it, unless you choose to keep the original.
  2. Your browser makes a random 256-bit key inside WebCrypto. It never leaves the tab.
  3. It encrypts the file in 4 MB chunks with AES-256-GCM. The name, type and real size go inside the encryption.
  4. It pads the file so we see a size bucket, not the exact size (the Padmé scheme: at least 1 KB, and at most 12% over for bigger files).
  5. It uploads the ciphertext and gets back a random id.
  6. It builds your link: the id before the #, a per-link secret after it. Browsers never send the part after # to a server; that's how the web works, not a promise we make.
  7. Link + password: the secret combines the link and your password ( rounds of PBKDF2-SHA256, OWASP's current figure). Neither alone opens it, and nobody can guess your password from our database alone, because we never see the link's part. After wrong passwords the link pauses, for longer each time.
  8. Password only: we hold the ciphertext, so a weak password could be guessed offline by someone with a copy of our database. Online guessing slows down after a few wrong tries. Use four or more random words.

How exits work

Our server counts opens, watches the clock, and deletes the ciphertext when the rule says so. Opening always takes a click, so link previews in chat apps don't use up an open.

We delete the ciphertext from our disk within a minute of its exit. If the exit was a download, we wait for it to finish (at most ). We keep a note that the link existed for , so we can tell you why it's gone. We never had the key, so any leftover bytes are noise.

Our database (never files) is backed up daily, and each copy is deleted after . It holds the rows our “What we know” page prints, and the wrapped key of every link still open when the copy was made, which opens nothing without the link's secret.

Our host doesn't keep snapshots of the server.

Our host (Hostinger) can take weekly snapshots of the whole server. We haven't switched them off, and we haven't yet confirmed in its panel how long it keeps them. A file that existed when one was taken stays in it, as ciphertext, until that snapshot is rotated out.

Disks can't be securely wiped, so deleted ciphertext can linger in free blocks. Without the key it's noise.

Every exit goes on the public record: we add it to SealLog on the Ootle at the next checkpoint (within ). It proves we said we deleted it, publicly and permanently. It can't prove we kept no copy.

Every stored file's ciphertext hash goes into SealLog on the Ootle too. Your browser can check it: a proof shows that the tree we committed to at that epoch contains your file's ciphertext hash. That shows what we committed to and when, not what we did with the file later.

Two locks

Programmable secrets: you write the rule and our server keeps the time. The Ootle carries keys we can't read and payments whose amounts only the payer and the payee see, and it keeps the receipts.

Lock 1 · The Ootle (the part we can't read)

  • Carries a file's key to one Tari address, sealed inside a stealth payment.
  • Moves money as stealth TARI, checked against chain state.
  • Keeps the receipts: public hashes of what we stored, and when it left.

(every exit)

Lock 2 · Our server (trust it, then check it)

  • Holds noise: ciphertext, never keys.
  • Keeps the clock: counts opens, watches expiry, deletes on cue.
  • Writes every exit to Lock 1, where anyone can check it.

Our server, in detail

Sees and stores
Ciphertext and its padded size; the hour an item arrived and when it left; expiry and view counts, and the time of a link's first open (shown only to its owner); the exit rule (link or password); for lockers: a random-looking locker id, a hash of its login key, the first and last active day, how many distinct days it was active, quota and packs bought; invoices (pack, amount, status) and the chain outputs that paid them; dead drops: the drop's Tari address, its label, its tag and daily counters; abuse reports; notes about ended links (state and day).
Sees, never stores
Every uploader's and every opener's IP address, live (so at the moment it happens we could link who uploaded a link with who opened it); the login key at sign-in; access tokens (hashed on arrival); which drop a file was left in, and the submitter's IP; the destination Tari address of every send, the sealed 125-byte blob, which locker sent it, and by timing which link it carries, kept in the job until sent and then scrubbed; that your locker submitted a transaction, not to whom; for dead-drop deliveries, the drop's address while the delivery is sent.
Never sees
File names, types, contents and real sizes; file keys, link secrets, passwords, seeds or any private key; your locker's Tari address, unless you make a drop or a relay send goes to it; who you send a file to, when your browser's wallet sends it (until its grant arrives, our relay sends it and learns the address while it's sent); which chain outputs your browser can open (everyone downloads the same copy of the chain); who shares with whom (there is no sharing inside mutis).

Everyone else

A network observer
That you talk to mutis, timing, and padded transfer sizes. Nothing inside TLS.
Someone with only the link's path (no #)
That a link exists, whether it needs a password, and its state; once it has ended, why and on what day, and its exit receipt: the file's id and exit hour, and through the log which checkpoint windows its arrival and exit fell in. Not the key, the contents, the name, the real size or the open count.
A view-key holder
Every delivery and payment to that address: amount, time and the memo's link text. Not the files locked to the locker, and it can't spend. The one exception: files a sender sealed with “The recipient uses another Ootle wallet” do open with the view key, and the send screen says so.
The public, through our live numbers
The free quota; our disk's size and free space, rounded; whether uploads or the Tari features are paused; how many lockers were active recently, once there are enough of them; SealLog's latest checkpoint; how many minutes ago someone last locked a file; and for up to 24 recent files, each at least an hour old, a coarse size and an exit rule, never whether one was opened, only when at least 8 exist in the last week. Nothing per person, but someone who knows a file's size and rule may recognise it there.
Our host (Hostinger)
Everything our server stores, plus the disk and any snapshots it keeps; in principle, live memory.

What we can't protect you from

  1. The person you send it to. They can save, screenshot or forward it.
  2. Anyone who gets the full link. The link is the key. Use Link + password (sent separately) or send it to one Tari address.
  3. Chat apps that preview links. Their servers see the full link. They can't use up a view (opening takes a click), but they could open it.
  4. Your own browser. Your browser history, and any history sync, can keep the full link, key included. We clear the key from the address bar once it's opened, but we can't reach your history. Extensions allowed to read your tabs can see the link. Clipboard history (and cloud clipboards) can keep a copied link.
  5. A compromised device or extension. Encryption in the browser can't beat malware in the browser.
  6. A malicious version of this website. It's the known weak spot of all web encryption: if our server were compromised, it could serve code that leaks keys. SRI only pins files to this page; it can't protect you from us. What can: our verify-deploy script and running your own copy.
  7. Metadata. We see a padded size (rounded up so the exact size stays hidden: at least 1 KB, and at most 12% over for anything bigger), the hour it arrived, when it leaves (rounded up to the hour when that's an hour or more away; “midnight” is your own local midnight, which shows your time zone), its exit rule and how often it's opened. Not the name, the type, the contents or the key. Our server sees your IP address while you're connected. We don't write it to disk: rate limits use a keyed hash, kept in memory, with a key that changes every day. A VPN or Tor hides your IP from us; links without a locker work in Tor Browser's Safer mode, and Tari features need WebAssembly.
  8. On-chain facts. The chain shows an output with a hidden amount, a hidden owner and an encrypted memo. The fee is public. Payments our relay sends all come from one wallet with the same fee, so someone watching could group them as “sent by mutis”, but not see to whom. Sends from your browser spend a grant from our sponsor wallet, then each send's change, so anyone reading the Ootle can link them to each other and to our sponsor wallet, but not see to whom or how much. SealLog shows roughly how many records (stored files plus exits) we write, in steps of 64, and when.
  9. Deletion you can't see. We delete on cue, and nothing can prove we kept no copy. Snapshots and free disk blocks are described above.
  10. Testnet resets. Esmeralda can be reset. Payments and on-chain records from before a reset are archived in our repository; files on our server are not affected.
  11. Legal requests. If compelled, we can hand over what we have: ciphertext, the columns on the “What we know” page, invoices. Not contents. Not keys.

How it's built

Built with AI coding agents under the author's direction. The design, threat model and crypto choices were reviewed by the author; the verification evidence is in the repository.

In your browser: our own modules, its WebCrypto, and one vendored library, Tari's official ootle-wasm , pinned by its hash. On the server: Python's standard library, Debian's Caddy and Node, and Tari's walletd for the chain. No npm install, anywhere.

No web fonts: every page uses the faces your system already has.
